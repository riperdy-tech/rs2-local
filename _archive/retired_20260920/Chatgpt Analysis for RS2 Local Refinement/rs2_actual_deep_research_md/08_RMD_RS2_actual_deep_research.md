## SECTION 0. Analysis Overview

ResMed is a high-quality sleep and respiratory care compounder with a strong global position in PAP devices, masks, accessories, cloud-connected sleep-health tools, and residential care software. The company benefits from a huge underdiagnosed obstructive sleep apnea market, recurring mask/resupply revenue, a strong installed base, and digital adherence infrastructure that matters to payors and home medical equipment providers.

The RS2 nomination is understandable. Q3 fiscal 2026 was strong: revenue rose 11% reported and 8% constant currency to $1.431 billion; non-GAAP gross margin expanded 290 bps to 62.8%; non-GAAP operating income rose 18%; non-GAAP EPS rose 21% to $2.86; and operating cash flow was $554 million for the quarter. For the nine months ended March 31, 2026, revenue rose 10% to $4.190 billion and non-GAAP EPS rose 17% to $8.22.

The central investment debate is no longer whether ResMed is a good company. It clearly is. The debate is:

- whether GLP-1 weight-loss drugs reduce OSA severity enough over time to hurt PAP demand;
- whether Philips' eventual U.S. recovery takes share back;
- whether reimbursement pressure returns through competitive bidding or other payor actions;
- whether margin expansion from supply-chain normalization and component savings is now close to peak;
- whether a roughly 19x FY26 non-GAAP EPS multiple is attractive enough.

At $209.63, RMD trades around 20.2x trailing GAAP EPS and about 18.8x-19.0x likely FY26 non-GAAP EPS. The balance sheet is excellent: $1.66 billion of cash and roughly $664 million of debt at March 31, 2026, or about $1.0 billion of net cash. Enterprise value is roughly $29.55 billion. Using expected FY26 revenue around $5.65-$5.70 billion, EV/revenue is about 5.2x.

Final view: **Hold / accumulate on weakness**. ResMed is a durable compounder, but current price offers more quality than mispricing. I would start small at current price, add below $195, and get more aggressive below $180 if Q4 confirms the current growth and margin trajectory.

Base value: $225-$235.  
Bull value: $270-$285.  
Bear value: $155-$165.

## SECTION 1. Macro Context

ResMed sits at the intersection of several large healthcare trends:

- rising prevalence of sleep apnea;
- obesity and cardiometabolic comorbidity;
- aging populations;
- home-based care;
- remote monitoring and cloud-connected therapy;
- pressure to reduce hospitalization and improve chronic-disease management;
- continued diagnosis gap in sleep-disordered breathing.

The macro demand case remains attractive. ResMed's 2025 10-K cites a Lancet Respiratory Medicine estimate that mild-to-severe OSA affects more than 936 million people globally, including 54 million Americans, and that more than 424 million globally may have moderate-to-severe sleep apnea. The same filing says fewer than 20% of U.S. OSA patients and 10% or less in other markets are diagnosed or treated. That underpenetration is the real long-term demand engine.

There are also material macro/policy risks:

- U.S. and international reimbursement can change.
- CMS competitive bidding could return.
- HME providers can face margin pressure and pass some of that pressure back to manufacturers.
- GLP-1 drugs could reduce OSA severity in some obese patients.
- Philips' consent-decree-limited U.S. position has benefited ResMed; a major Philips recovery could alter share dynamics.

For now, the data point most relevant to GLP-1 is mixed but more favorable than feared. Management has repeatedly argued that GLP-1 use can increase diagnosis and PAP initiation. ResMed has disclosed claims-analysis evidence suggesting OSA patients prescribed GLP-1 drugs are more likely to initiate PAP therapy and have higher resupply behavior. The long-term unknown is whether sustained weight loss materially reduces PAP need in later cohorts.

Macro verdict: **structurally favorable demand, with GLP-1 and reimbursement risk as the major offset.**

## SECTION 1.5. Base Rate Check

The base rate for dominant medtech companies with recurring consumables is good. These businesses often compound when they combine:

- strong installed base;
- clinical necessity;
- recurring supplies;
- product innovation;
- distribution relationships;
- reimbursement support;
- compliance and connectivity tools.

ResMed checks most of those boxes.

The weaker base-rate category is durable medical equipment exposed to reimbursement resets. PAP devices and masks are not immune to CMS or commercial payor changes. Another base-rate caution is that medical device leaders can enjoy unusually strong periods when a competitor is constrained, then face tougher comparisons when that competitor normalizes. Philips' recall and consent decree have helped create a market-share window for ResMed and smaller entrants; the company itself discloses uncertainty about the timing and impact of Philips' substantial U.S. return.

RMD's base-rate profile is therefore:

- better than commodity medical device;
- better than one-time capital-equipment medtech;
- less pristine than software because reimbursement and hardware supply chain matter;
- less risky than biotech because there is no single trial binary.

Base-rate verdict: **above-average healthcare compounder, but not immune to reimbursement and competitive cycles.**

## SECTION 3.5. Second-Order Effects

1. **GLP-1 may increase diagnosis before reducing severity.** Weight-loss drugs bring obese patients into medical care. Some will be screened for OSA and start PAP. The long-run question is whether durable weight loss eventually reduces device dependence.

2. **Masks matter more than devices for quality of revenue.** Device sales are important, but mask/accessory growth shows installed-base health. Q3 mask and other sales rose 12% constant currency.

3. **Philips is both a risk and proof of ResMed's execution.** Philips' recall created share opportunity. ResMed used that opportunity to expand. The risk is future competitive normalization.

4. **Residential Care Software is strategically useful but not the main growth engine.** Software provides home-care workflow exposure and sticky customer relationships, but Q3 constant-currency growth was only 4%.

5. **Margin expansion may be mature.** Q3 margin benefited from component cost improvements and manufacturing/logistics efficiencies. Some of that is structural, but the year-over-year comparison gets harder once supply-chain normalization is fully reflected.

Second-order verdict: **positive, but requires careful monitoring of GLP-1 and margin normalization.**

## SECTION 7. Risks

**1. GLP-1 risk**

GLP-1 drugs may reduce obesity and OSA severity for some patients. ResMed discloses this as a potential competitive/alternative treatment risk.

**2. Philips competitive return**

Philips remains under a consent decree related to its recall. ResMed cannot predict timing or impact of Philips' substantial return. A strong return could reduce ResMed growth or pricing power.

**3. Reimbursement risk**

Medicare, Medicaid, commercial insurers, and international payors can reduce coverage or reimbursement. CMS competitive bidding could return.

**4. HME customer economics**

ResMed sells largely through HME providers, health systems, and sleep clinics. If their reimbursement is pressured, ResMed may face indirect price pressure.

**5. Margin normalization**

Recent gross margin expansion reflects manufacturing/logistics efficiency and component cost improvement. Those benefits may not repeat indefinitely.

**6. Supply-chain risk**

The company relies on components, magnets, semiconductors, and global manufacturing/logistics. Disruption can compress margins or delay shipments.

**7. Software competition and cyber risk**

Residential Care Software faces competition, low switching willingness in some accounts, data privacy risk, and rapidly evolving technology/AI expectations.

**8. Regulatory and compliance risk**

Medical devices, diagnostics, billing, fraud and abuse, privacy, quality systems, and international regulations all create compliance exposure.

Risk rating: **medium-low for medtech, medium in absolute terms.**
