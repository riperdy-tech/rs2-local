## SECTION 0. Analysis Overview

Laureate Education is a scaled private higher-education operator focused on Mexico and Peru. The post-divestiture company is much simpler than old Laureate: five institutions, two country segments, roughly 500,000 students, and a capital-light service model that converts a meaningful portion of EBITDA into cash after campus investment.

The RS2 signal is understandable. LAUR combines:

- visible enrollment growth;
- strong private higher-education demand in markets where public capacity is constrained;
- high adjusted EBITDA margins for an education operator;
- a balance sheet that is close to net-neutral after cash and debt;
- active buybacks that are shrinking the share count;
- a business model with recurring tuition-like revenue, but not software-like lock-in.

The main issue is valuation discipline. At roughly $38.20, LAUR is no longer obviously cheap. Using Q1 2026 balance sheet data, enterprise value is roughly $5.41 billion after adding $215.8 million of gross debt and subtracting $157.4 million of cash. Against 2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance of $583 million to $593 million, the stock trades near 9.2x current-year adjusted EBITDA. Against updated adjusted EPS guidance of $2.00 to $2.08, it trades at about 18.7x the midpoint. That is fair-to-full for an emerging-market, FX-exposed education operator, even one with a strong moat in its local niches.

My conclusion: **quality compounder, but the stock should be accumulated only on weakness rather than chased after the screen flag.** If already owned, it is a hold. For new capital, the better setup is a staged starter under $36 and stronger adds if the stock gives back into the low $30s without a deterioration in enrollment intake.

Base expected value: $41 to $43 over 12-18 months.  
Bull case: $50+ if Peru online growth and Mexico stabilization persist while FX remains favorable.  
Bear case: $27 to $30 if Mexico macro weakens, FX reverses, or private education regulation becomes more hostile.

## SECTION 1. Macro Context

Laureate's macro context is not a generic U.S. education story. It is a Mexico/Peru consumer-income, currency, regulation, and labor-market story reported in U.S. dollars.

The attractive side:

- Private higher education has structural demand because public university capacity is limited and often selective.
- The company emphasizes job-oriented programs, including health sciences, IT/engineering, and business.
- Peru is showing better enrollment momentum than Mexico, helped by working-adult online programs.
- Mexico remains a large market with meaningful private-sector participation, but the macro backdrop is softer.

The harder side:

- Reported U.S.-dollar results are sensitive to the Mexican peso and Peruvian sol.
- Education is regulated locally, with licensing and program approvals central to the business.
- The consumer is exposed to inflation, unemployment, political uncertainty, and household affordability.
- The stock's U.S. investor base may apply a discount when Latin American currency risk becomes more visible.

The World Bank's 2026 macro view is constructive but not euphoric. Mexico growth is projected around 1.3% in 2026, with a gradual recovery but lingering trade-policy uncertainty. Peru's economy expanded 3.4% in 2025 and is expected to grow around 2.7% in 2026. Those conditions support Laureate's base case, but not an aggressive multiple expansion case.

Company guidance uses assumed FX of MXN 17.95 and PEN 3.45 per U.S. dollar for most of 2026. That matters because LAUR's reported revenue and EBITDA can look better or worse depending on translation, even when local student economics are stable. The company disclosed in its 2025 10-K that a hypothetical 10% adverse move in average annual FX rates would have reduced 2025 revenue by about $170.2 million and adjusted EBITDA by about $57.3 million. That is a large sensitivity relative to current valuation.

Macro score: **mixed-positive**. Structural education demand is attractive, but FX and country risk prevent a clean high-multiple U.S. compounder framing.

## SECTION 1.5. Base Rate Check

The base rate for for-profit or private education companies is not automatically favorable. Many education operators look stable until one of four things breaks:

1. Regulatory permission to operate or expand.
2. Student affordability and enrollment growth.
3. Brand/reputation and job-outcome credibility.
4. Marketing efficiency.

Laureate is better positioned than the weakest historical for-profit education examples because it is not dependent on U.S. federal student-aid mechanics and because it has strong local brands in Mexico and Peru. It also operates at meaningful scale, with the company reporting 50+ campuses, roughly 498,000 students, more than 33,000 faculty/staff, and 30.5% adjusted EBITDA margin for 2025.

Still, the base-rate caution is real:

- Education brands can suffer from sector-wide reputational damage.
- Online growth can attract new digital competitors.
- Regulatory frameworks can shift after elections or public pressure.
- Enrollment momentum tends to be cyclical around employment, family income, and consumer confidence.

The correct base-rate adjustment is to give LAUR credit for local scale and execution, but not to value it like an unregulated U.S. subscription platform. The business can compound, but it deserves a discount for geography, FX, and policy risk.

Base-rate verdict: **above-average operator in a mixed base-rate industry**.

## SECTION 2. Business Quality

Laureate's business quality is high relative to most small- and mid-cap consumer-service companies.

The core business:

- Mexico: UVM and UNITEC, with total enrollment of 269,400 at December 31, 2025.
- Peru: UPC, UPN, and CIBERTEC, with total enrollment of 228,300 at December 31, 2025.
- Segments: Mexico and Peru.
- Programs: campus-based, hybrid, and fully online.
- Target categories: traditional students, working adults, technical/vocational learners, and professional-oriented degree seekers.

The moat is local rather than technological. It comes from:

- brand recognition;
- regulatory licenses and program approvals;
- physical campus networks;
- curriculum scale;
- employer familiarity;
- student acquisition infrastructure;
- local operating experience.

Enrollment trends are strong. For full-year 2025, total new enrollments rose 8%, with Peru up 13% and Mexico up 5%. Total enrollments were up 5%, with Peru up 7% and Mexico up 4%. In Q1 2026, company-wide new enrollments rose 9% and total enrollments rose 6%; Peru was again the standout, while Mexico was positive but more subdued.

Profit quality is also good. Full-year 2025 revenue was $1.7019 billion, up 9% reported and 8% organic constant currency. Adjusted EBITDA was $518.9 million, up 15%. That implies a 30.5% adjusted EBITDA margin, unusually strong for an education-services company with physical campuses.

The weakness: the business is not asset-free. Campus upgrades, new campuses, online infrastructure, and quality requirements all require capital. 2025 capex was about $103.0 million, up from $71.9 million in 2024. That is manageable against $518.9 million of EBITDA, but it means adjusted EBITDA overstates true distributable cash.

Business-quality score: **8/10**. Strong local platforms, good margins, and visible demand, offset by regulatory/FX exposure and reinvestment needs.

## SECTION 3.5. Second-Order Effects

Several second-order dynamics matter more than the headline P/E:

1. **Buybacks can create EPS resilience.** LAUR repurchased about $217 million of common stock in 2025 and about $105 million in Q1 2026. The company had approximately $76 million remaining authorization as of March 31, 2026. If free cash flow remains strong, repurchases can support EPS even if revenue growth slows.

2. **FX can turn operational mediocrity into reported strength, or the reverse.** Q1 2026 revenue rose 15% reported but only 1% constant currency. That is a huge difference. A bullish stock reaction to reported numbers can reverse if peso/sol translation turns unfavorable.

3. **Peru online growth changes the margin mix.** Working-adult online programs can expand addressable market and may carry attractive economics if completion, student satisfaction, and regulatory acceptance hold. But online education is also more contestable and reputation-sensitive.

4. **Campus investments can defend the moat but pressure cash conversion.** New campuses and upgrades make the brands more attractive, yet capex rose materially in 2025. If growth requires much more capital than expected, FCF quality could disappoint.

5. **Regulatory permanence can reduce risk, but never eliminates it.** Peru's 2024 change making university licenses permanent is favorable, but institutions still need to meet basic quality conditions. Mexico's REVOE structure remains program- and campus-specific. The moat exists partly because regulation is complex; the same complexity is also a risk.

Second-order verdict: **positive if execution continues, but valuation already prices several of these benefits.**

## SECTION 5.5. Behavioral

The behavioral trap is to see:

- 100 RS2 score;
- strong enrollment growth;
- high EBITDA margin;
- buybacks;
- simple story;

and then skip the price discipline. That would be the wrong lesson.

The second trap is to over-penalize the Q1 adjusted EBITDA loss. Q1 is seasonally low, and the company explicitly identified academic-calendar timing. The right interpretation is neither "Q1 proves the business broke" nor "ignore Q1 completely." The right check is whether Q2/Q3 and enrollment data confirm the full-year path.

The third trap is currency blindness. U.S. investors often treat reported dollar growth as operating growth. For LAUR, reported and constant-currency growth can diverge sharply. In Q1 2026, reported revenue growth was 15%, while constant-currency revenue growth was 1%. A disciplined analyst must underwrite local-currency demand, not just U.S.-dollar translated results.

Behavioral stance:

- Do not chase strength.
- Do not panic on seasonal noise.
- Do require a margin of safety for FX and regulation.
- Do track local enrollment more than headline reported revenue.

## SECTION 7. Risks

**1. FX translation risk**

A stronger U.S. dollar reduces reported revenue and earnings. The company's 10-K sensitivity shows a 10% adverse average FX move would have reduced 2025 adjusted EBITDA by about $57.3 million.

**2. Mexico macro weakness**

Mexico is a large portion of enrollment and revenue. World Bank and other macro sources point to slower 2026 growth and trade-policy uncertainty. Student affordability could weaken if household conditions deteriorate.

**3. Peru growth normalization**

Peru is currently the growth engine. If the primary intake slows or online working-adult programs lose momentum, the consolidated growth narrative weakens.

**4. Regulation and licensing**

Mexico's REVOEs are program- and campus-specific. Peru still requires basic quality conditions even after making university licenses permanent. Any adverse regulatory change could affect program launches, campus expansion, or online delivery.

**5. Online education execution**

Online programs can expand the addressable market, but they also invite competition, infrastructure risk, and employer/regulator scrutiny.

**6. Capex creep**

Capex rose to $103 million in 2025. If campus expansion and upgrades require sustained higher spending, free cash flow may disappoint.

**7. Reputation**

The company discloses that reputational harm to the private or for-profit education sector can affect student demand and regulatory scrutiny. This is difficult to forecast but important.

**8. Capital allocation**

Buybacks help when shares are undervalued. If repurchases continue aggressively at full valuation, the benefit declines.

Risk rating: **medium**. The business is good, but the geography and regulatory context make the risk profile meaningfully higher than a domestic education-services screen might imply.
