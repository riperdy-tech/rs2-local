## Section 1. Macro Context

The macro/thematic backdrop is extremely favorable. AI/data-center power demand has made firm, clean power much more valuable, and the U.S. government has been moving aggressively to support nuclear supply chains and advanced reactor deployment. Reuters reported this week that the DOE announced **$17.5B** in conditional loans to support nuclear supply-chain procurement for new reactors, while another DOE initiative is opening federal sites and regulatory support for advanced reactor rollout. ([Reuters][3])

But OKLO is also highly rate- and capital-market-sensitive. It needs approvals, construction execution, project financing, supply-chain reliability, fuel availability, and customer conversion before it can generate material cash flow.

**Macro alignment:** **Very positive for narrative; still risky for execution.**

## Section 2. Business Quality

Oklo is developing fast-fission “Aurora powerhouse” reactors and intends to **build, own, and operate** the plants, selling electricity and heat through PPAs rather than selling reactor designs. The company says Aurora is designed for **15–75 MWe** and potentially **100 MWe+** versions, using fresh, recycled, or down-blended nuclear fuel. ([SEC][1])

**Moat direction:** **Widening on paper, unvalidated in cash flow.**
The business could become very high quality if Aurora units are licensed, built, and financed repeatedly. Today, however, the moat is mostly technical/regulatory optionality rather than proven operating economics.

## Section 3.5. Second-Order Effects

**Competitive response:** TerraPower, X-energy, Kairos, NuScale, GE Hitachi, Westinghouse/AP1000, Holtec, Radiant, Newcleo, and large utilities are all competing for nuclear deployment attention, customers, supply chain, labor, fuel, and policy support. Reuters reported that Kairos, TerraPower, and X-energy are already moving next-generation nuclear projects into construction under DOE cost-sharing support, with customers including Google, Meta, and Dow. ([Reuters][7])

**Reflexivity:** OKLO’s stock price matters a lot. A high stock price lets it raise capital with less dilution, fund early site work, and strengthen credibility. A falling stock price could increase dilution, slow execution, and weaken customer/project-finance confidence.

**Customer behavior:** Data centers increasingly want reliable, firm, clean power. That supports OKLO’s TAM, but customers may prefer existing nuclear, gas, or larger advanced-reactor developers with clearer near-term delivery.

**Network dynamics:** No true network effect yet. The future flywheel would be learning-curve effects: first units reduce regulatory, construction, and financing risk for later units.

## Section 7. Risks & Catalysts

### Top 3 Risks

1. **Licensing risk:** Oklo still needs regulatory approvals for design, construction, and operation; the company states it is uncertain when, if at all, it will obtain NRC approvals. ([SEC][1])
2. **First-of-a-kind construction risk:** Oklo itself says early powerhouses, including INL, may face unexpected costs and construction delays, and first reactors will include unique testing-related costs. ([SEC][1])
3. **Dilution / funding risk:** Oklo has cash, but it still expects **$350–450M** of investing cash use in 2026 and is continuing to pursue capital raising at the corporate and asset level. ([SEC][1])

### Top 3 Catalysts

1. Updated NRC application progress or additional DOE authorization milestones.
2. Meta/Pike County Ohio project moves from prepayment/support into clearer binding project economics.
3. HALEU supply and fuel-fabrication milestones reduce one of the largest bottlenecks for deployment.

**Highest-impact bear scenario:** OKLO burns cash and raises more equity while licensing/construction slips, causing the stock to trade closer to cash value plus discounted optionality.
