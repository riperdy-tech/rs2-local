## SECTION 0. Analysis Overview

First Solar is one of the more interesting RS2 candidates because it combines industrial execution, a strong U.S. manufacturing position, a large contracted backlog, a net cash balance sheet, and enormous profitability support from Section 45X advanced manufacturing tax credits. The stock looks statistically cheap at about 14.5x trailing EPS and roughly 8.2x 2026 guided adjusted EBITDA after net cash. But the quality of that EBITDA is unusual: a very large portion is policy-enabled.

Q1 2026 was strong. Net sales increased 24% year over year to $1.04 billion, diluted EPS increased 65% to $3.22, adjusted EBITDA was $520 million, gross cash was $2.4 billion, net cash was $2.0 billion, and contracted backlog was 47.9 GW. Management reaffirmed 2026 guidance for $4.9 billion to $5.2 billion of net sales, $2.6 billion to $2.8 billion of adjusted EBITDA, and $1.7 billion to $2.3 billion of year-end net cash.

My conclusion is Buy / staged accumulate, not reckless buy. The current valuation compensates for some solar-cycle and policy risk, and First Solar is strategically advantaged versus Chinese crystalline silicon supply chains. However, investors must monitor backlog conversion, 45X monetization, tariffs, project-credit demand effects, Series 7 warranty exposure, and policy changes. This is a good risk/reward, but it is not a sleep-well industrial compounder.

## SECTION 1. Macro Context

The macro context for First Solar is unusually policy-dependent. Utility-scale solar demand is driven by power demand, grid interconnection, project economics, tax credits, tariffs, domestic content rules, supply-chain restrictions, and buyer preference for bankable module supply. Electricity demand from data centers, electrification, industrial reshoring, and grid decarbonization supports long-term solar demand. At the same time, solar project economics can change quickly when tax credits, tariffs, interest rates, or permitting timelines change.

First Solar benefits from two important macro currents. First, U.S. buyers want supply-chain certainty and non-China exposure. First Solar's CdTe thin-film technology and U.S. manufacturing footprint are differentiated in an industry dominated by crystalline silicon supply chains with heavy China exposure. Second, U.S. manufacturing policy has created material economics for domestic module production.

The negative macro factor is that policy can cut both ways. First Solar's 2025 10-K explicitly discusses H.R.1 risks around clean electricity credits, FEOC restrictions, and Section 45X eligibility. Industry summaries of current clean energy law changes also highlight tighter timing and eligibility rules for solar project credits. If project tax-credit economics weaken, customer demand or pricing can suffer even if First Solar's own manufacturing credits remain valuable.

## SECTION 1.5. Base Rate Check

The base rate for solar manufacturers is poor if treated as a commodity manufacturing category. Historically, module makers have suffered from overcapacity, price pressure, trade disputes, technology shifts, and boom-bust subsidy cycles. Many have looked cheap at peak earnings and then destroyed capital.

First Solar deserves a better base rate than generic solar manufacturers. It has differentiated CdTe technology, a domestic manufacturing advantage, a long contracted backlog, a net cash balance sheet, disciplined contracting, and U.S. policy support. It is also not competing purely on commodity crystalline silicon module economics.

The base-rate warning remains serious: when a large portion of economics comes from government credits, normalized earnings power is harder to underwrite. The investment works if First Solar can convert backlog, protect pricing, execute capacity expansion, and retain enough policy benefit. It fails if credits disappear, customers renegotiate, backlog erodes, or module pricing falls faster than cost and credit advantages.

## SECTION 2. Business Quality

First Solar designs, manufactures, and sells cadmium telluride thin-film photovoltaic modules. It operates as a single business segment and sells primarily to developers, independent power producers, utilities, commercial and industrial buyers, and other system owners/operators.

The business quality has several strong points:

- Technology differentiation: CdTe thin-film modules are not the same supply chain as conventional crystalline silicon modules.
- Domestic manufacturing: U.S. capacity is strategically valuable under domestic-content, FEOC, tariff, and procurement preferences.
- Backlog visibility: contracted backlog was 47.9 GW as of March 31, 2026.
- Balance sheet: Q1 2026 net cash was $2.0 billion.
- Scale: 2025 production was 16.1 GW and sales were 17.5 GW; Q1 2026 production was 4.3 GW and volume sold was 3.8 GW.

The quality limitations are just as important:

- Earnings are highly exposed to policy credits.
- Module selling prices can decline.
- Manufacturing ramps can create underutilization/start-up costs.
- Warranty and product-quality events can be costly.
- Customer concentration and contract disputes can matter.

This is a better-than-average solar manufacturer, but the business quality is not equivalent to a high-recurring-revenue software or medical device platform.

## SECTION 2.5. Adjusted Financials

2025 net sales were $5.219 billion, up 24% from 2024, primarily due to a 24% increase in module volume sold to third parties. Gross profit was $2.120 billion, and gross margin was 40.6%, down from 44.2% in 2024 because of higher costs tied to U.S.-produced mix, warehousing, duties/tariffs, and logistics, partly offset by higher Section 45X credits. First Solar recognized $1.6 billion of Section 45X credits as a reduction to cost of sales in 2025.

Q1 2026 net sales were $1.044 billion, up 23.6% from $844.6 million in Q1 2025. The increase was driven by a 30.9% increase in volume sold to third parties, partially offset by lower average selling price per watt due to more volume sold in India. Gross profit was $486.1 million, and gross margin was 46.6%, up from 40.8%. Net income was $346.6 million, or $3.22 diluted EPS.

Q1 gross margin was materially helped by Section 45X. Cost of sales benefited from $117.9 million more credits year over year, and the Q1 presentation shows $418 million of Section 45X tax credits in the quarter. This is not a footnote; it is central to earnings.

Balance sheet strength is real. Q1 2026 gross cash was $2.4 billion and net cash was $2.0 billion. Long-term debt plus current maturities was only about $328 million, mostly tied to India facility financing and related working-capital arrangements. That gives First Solar meaningful resilience versus weaker solar manufacturers.

## SECTION 3. Valuation

At $224.57 and market cap of approximately $24.17 billion, subtracting Q1 2026 net cash of about $2.0 billion gives a rough enterprise value of $22.17 billion.

Using 2026 guidance:

- Net sales guidance: $4.9 billion to $5.2 billion; midpoint $5.05 billion.
- Adjusted EBITDA guidance: $2.6 billion to $2.8 billion; midpoint $2.7 billion.
- EV / midpoint sales: approximately 4.4x.
- EV / midpoint adjusted EBITDA: approximately 8.2x.
- Market cap / trailing EPS: approximately 14.5x using the live market EPS figure of $15.48.

Those multiples look attractive, especially for a company with net cash and contracted backlog. The valuation becomes less simple when considering that 2026 guidance includes $2.10 billion to $2.19 billion of Section 45X tax credits. If one heavily discounts policy-enabled earnings, the stock is less cheap. If one believes 45X is durable enough through the relevant capacity-payback period, the stock is attractively valued.

Base-case fair value: $260 to $300, using 9x to 10x adjusted EBITDA after net cash and applying a policy-risk discount.

Bull-case fair value: $360 to $420, if backlog conversion remains strong, U.S. policy support persists, 45X monetization remains reliable, and demand from power/data-center growth supports pricing.

Bear-case fair value: $150 to $175, if project-credit changes reduce demand, 45X rules become materially less favorable, backlog cancellations/renegotiations rise, or module ASPs compress.

## SECTION 3.5. Second-Order Effects

First, Section 45X credits make the economics powerful but also fragile. First Solar expects approximately 17 cents per watt of credit for U.S.-produced modules sold to third parties. That can overwhelm normal manufacturing margin. It also means investors must treat tax-credit eligibility, transferability, discount rates, FEOC restrictions, and buyer demand as core operating variables.

Second, backlog quality matters more than backlog size. A 47.9 GW backlog with $14.4 billion aggregate transaction price is valuable, but the implied average price is only one part of the story. Contract enforceability, customer credit quality, delivery timing, escalation terms, and cancellation/renegotiation risk matter.

Third, tariffs can be both positive and negative. Tariffs on imported crystalline silicon modules can improve First Solar's competitive position, but tariffs on raw materials and inputs can increase costs. Q1 2026 guidance assumptions included Section 122 tariffs, Section 232 aluminum/steel tariffs, and no IEEPA tariff recovery.

Fourth, domestic-content and FEOC rules may help First Solar relative to foreign supply chains, but they can also complicate customer project qualification. A project that cannot use credits may be less willing or able to pay premium module prices.

Fifth, warranty credibility is important. Series 7 manufacturing issues are not thesis-ending, but they remind investors that manufacturing quality is a real risk, not an abstract disclosure.

## SECTION 4. Scenarios

Bull case: U.S. power demand remains strong, project developers prioritize secure domestic/non-China supply, 45X credits remain monetizable, backlog converts without major renegotiation, and First Solar executes its U.S. capacity ramps. Adjusted EBITDA remains above $2.7 billion, net cash grows, and the market assigns a 11x to 13x EBITDA multiple. Fair value: $360 to $420.

Base case: 2026 guidance is achieved, backlog conversion remains solid, 45X credits remain materially intact, but investors continue to apply a policy and solar-cycle discount. The stock deserves 9x to 10x adjusted EBITDA plus net cash. Fair value: $260 to $300.

Bear case: project demand weakens after tax-credit changes, customers push back on pricing or timing, backlog shrinks, Section 45X eligibility becomes more restrictive, and tariffs/input costs reduce gross margin. The market applies a 5x to 6x EBITDA multiple to lower normalized earnings. Fair value: $150 to $175.

Stress case: policy shock plus backlog cancellations plus product-quality costs. Because First Solar has net cash, this is unlikely to become a solvency crisis, but equity value could compress sharply.

## SECTION 4.5. Horizon Arbitrage

Short-term investors will focus on quarterly EPS, Section 45X recognition, backlog movement, and policy headlines. This can create large stock swings, because reported earnings are very sensitive to credit recognition and module shipment timing.

Longer-term investors can focus on whether First Solar's domestic manufacturing advantage remains strategically scarce. If the U.S. continues to value non-China solar supply, First Solar's capacity and backlog should remain valuable even through volatile policy cycles.

The horizon arbitrage is real but uncomfortable: the stock is cheap on near-term earnings, but investors must decide how much of those earnings are durable. A patient investor who accepts policy volatility can be paid well; a short-term trader can get hurt by headlines.

## SECTION 5.5. Behavioral

The bullish trap is treating all EBITDA as normal industrial EBITDA. It is not. Section 45X is central to First Solar's current margin structure.

The bearish trap is dismissing all policy-enabled earnings as fake. U.S. policy can persist long enough to create real cash, fund capacity, and strengthen competitive position. First Solar already monetized large 45X credit amounts in prior years and has substantial net cash.

The disciplined view is to own the asymmetry, but size it as a policy-sensitive industrial, not a permanently advantaged compounder.

## SECTION 7. Risks

Policy risk is the largest risk. 45X credits, clean electricity project credits, FEOC rules, domestic-content requirements, and tariff regimes all affect First Solar economics and demand.

Tax-credit monetization risk matters. First Solar has sold credits at discounts in the past. If transfer markets become less liquid or eligibility is challenged, cash realization can fall short of accounting value.

Backlog risk is material. The company had 47.9 GW contracted as of March 31, 2026, but the 2025 10-K also disclosed backlog reductions from terminations. Customers may seek renegotiations if economics change.

Module pricing risk is structural. Solar modules remain exposed to global overcapacity and price competition, even if First Solar is differentiated.

Manufacturing ramp risk exists. 2026 guidance includes underutilization costs of $115 million to $155 million and production start-up costs of $110 million to $120 million.

Warranty risk is visible. Series 7 manufacturing issues created estimated loss ranges and specific warranty liabilities. Quality control must remain tight as capacity expands.

Input cost and tariff risk matter. Tariffs can help competitiveness versus imports but hurt cost structure through raw materials and components.

## SECTION 7.5. Correlation Risk

FSLR correlates with clean energy equities, solar policy headlines, interest rates, utility-scale project economics, energy-transition sentiment, and U.S.-China trade narratives. It may also trade with industrial reshoring themes and data-center power demand enthusiasm.

It is not low-beta. Even with net cash and backlog, the stock can gap on tax policy, tariff, or election-related headlines.

Portfolio implication: FSLR can be a useful diversifier, but it should be sized with volatility expectations similar to policy-sensitive cyclicals.

## SECTION 9. Red Team

The bearish case is powerful: First Solar's earnings are inflated by temporary policy credits, backlog is vulnerable to project economics, solar module pricing is structurally competitive, and 2026 revenue guidance disappointed investors because sales are not growing as fast as headline earnings. If 45X credits are discounted or impaired, normalized EBITDA could be far lower than reported guidance.

Another bear point is that First Solar's valuation appears cheap only because near-term earnings are at a policy-supported peak. A low P/E can be a trap if credits phase down, transfer discounts widen, or future backlog carries lower prices.

The bullish response is that the cash is real if credits are monetized, domestic manufacturing scarcity is strategically valuable, and First Solar has a balance sheet that gives it staying power. The right answer is not to ignore the risk; it is to require a lower multiple and disciplined sizing. At $225, the market is offering that lower multiple.
