## SECTION 0. Analysis Overview

G. Willi-Food International is an Israeli importer, developer, marketer, and distributor of kosher food products. It sells more than 650 food products through over 1,500 customers and 3,000 selling points in Israel and abroad [Actual]. The company is small, profitable, cash-rich, and operationally resilient, but the stock is highly illiquid and exposed to Israel-specific geopolitical, currency, and logistics risk.

Q1 2026 was a strong operating quarter. Sales increased 8.3% year over year to NIS 157.0M / $49.6M [Actual]. Gross profit increased 9.7% to NIS 49.0M / $15.5M [Actual]. Operating profit was NIS 20.0M / $6.3M [Actual], representing a 12.8% operating margin [Actual]. Net profit increased 3.0% to NIS 20.1M / $6.3M [Actual], or NIS 1.44 / $0.5 per share [Actual]. Net cash from operating activities was NIS 22.4M / $7.1M [Actual].

FY2025 was also strong. Sales increased 6.0% to NIS 610.6M / $191.4M [Actual], gross profit increased 8.4% to NIS 174.8M / $54.9M [Actual], operating profit increased 35.6% to NIS 74.4M / $23.3M [Actual], and net profit increased 28.6% to NIS 90.4M / $28.3M [Actual]. The company ended Q1 2026 with NIS 274.1M / $86.6M in cash and securities [Actual] and NIS 653.8M / $206.6M shareholders' equity [Actual].

At roughly $32.5-$33.0 per share and about $447M-$456M market cap [Actual], WILC trades around 15.8x-16.1x FY2025 net income [Estimate], around 17x Q1 annualized net income [Estimate], about 2.2x book value [Estimate], and about 2.3x annualized Q1 sales [Estimate]. That valuation is reasonable for a high-margin food distributor with net cash, but it is not cheap enough to ignore microcap liquidity, country risk, and limited disclosure depth.

RS2 conclusion: WILC is a Hold / watchlist. The business quality is better than average for a microcap, but the stock price already capitalizes much of the quality. Better entry would be below $27-$28 or after evidence that the new refrigerated logistics center expands margins and revenue without execution disruption.

## SECTION 1. Macro Context

WILC is exposed primarily to Israeli food demand, import costs, currency movements, regional logistics, and geopolitical risk. Food demand is more defensive than discretionary categories, which gives the business some resilience.

The macro risk is location. The company explicitly cites changes in political, economic, and military conditions in Israel, recent war in Israel, regional instability, and Red Sea shipping disruptions as risks [Actual]. Q1 management noted that delays from the ongoing war situation pushed expected completion and start of operations for the new refrigerated logistics center to Q4 2026 [Actual].

Currency is meaningful. WILC imports food products and reports in NIS. Management said Q1 2026 gross margin benefited from NIS appreciation against the U.S. dollar and euro, which improved purchase prices [Actual]. That creates upside in favorable FX periods and margin risk if NIS weakens.

Food inflation has two-sided effects. Higher global food prices can raise revenue but pressure gross margin and consumer demand if pass-through is incomplete. WILC's 2025 and Q1 2026 margins improved partly through better commercial terms, product mix, and favorable FX [Actual].

Macro score: defensive demand but elevated country/logistics/currency risk.

## SECTION 1.5. Base Rate Check

Base rate for small food distributors: these businesses can be stable and cash-generative but often earn modest valuation multiples because scale, supplier/customer concentration, logistics, and working capital are hard to compound rapidly.

Base rate for microcap international ADRs: even profitable companies often deserve liquidity and governance discounts. Low trading volume can impair entry/exit and increase volatility unrelated to fundamentals.

Base rate for cash-rich microcaps: balance-sheet cash protects downside, but markets may discount cash if capital allocation, related-party governance, or minority-shareholder treatment is uncertain.

Base rate for logistics investments: new warehouses/logistics centers can improve capacity and margins, but completion delays, cost overruns, and ramp inefficiencies are common.

Base-rate conclusion: WILC is higher quality than many microcaps, but the right multiple should reflect liquidity, country risk, and limited growth visibility.

## SECTION 2. Business Quality

WILC is a specialized importer and distributor of kosher food products. The company markets a broad product portfolio and has relationships with large retail chains, private supermarkets, wholesalers, and institutional customers [Actual]. Its scale is modest, but its niche focus and distribution network create some defensibility.

Profitability is strong for the category. Q1 2026 gross margin was 31.2% [Actual], up from 30.8% in Q1 2025 [Actual]. Operating margin was 12.8% [Actual]. FY2025 operating margin was 12.2% [Actual], up from 9.5% in 2024 [Actual]. These are attractive margins for a food importer/distributor.

The balance sheet is a quality point. Q1 2026 cash and securities were NIS 274.1M / $86.6M [Actual], and shareholders' equity was NIS 653.8M / $206.6M [Actual]. A cash-rich balance sheet provides resilience against war/logistics disruptions and working-capital needs.

Growth is moderate but consistent. Sales increased 6.0% in 2025 [Actual] and 8.3% in Q1 2026 [Actual]. Management attributes growth to better inventory/product availability and growing demand [Actual]. The new refrigerated logistics center could support frozen/chilled categories and improve scale, but completion is now expected in Q4 2026 [Actual].

Business-quality caveat: financial income is material. Q1 2026 financial income was NIS 5.7M [Actual], including NIS 2.9M from securities revaluation and NIS 2.8M from interest/dividend income [Actual]. FY2025 financial income was NIS 42.2M [Actual]. Net profit therefore includes investment-portfolio contribution, not just food operations.

Business quality score: 10.5/15. Strong margins, cash, niche positioning, and resilience; deductions for scale, liquidity, geopolitical risk, and financial-income dependence.

## SECTION 3.5. Second-Order Effects

The logistics center is the main second-order lever. First-order effect: more refrigerated capacity. Second-order effects: entry into more frozen/chilled categories, better inventory availability, improved service levels, and possible margin expansion. The risk is execution delay, cost escalation, or underutilization.

FX has second-order margin impact. NIS strength helped purchase costs in Q1 [Actual]. If NIS weakens against USD/EUR, reported gross margin could reverse even if demand is stable.

Securities portfolio has second-order earnings volatility. Positive revaluation gains boost net income and cash perception, but market declines could reduce financial income and headline EPS.

War/logistics risk has second-order customer effects. If regional disruption raises shipping costs or delays product availability, customers may shift suppliers or WILC may need to hold more inventory, increasing working capital.

## SECTION 4. Scenarios

Bull case:

Sales grow 8%-10% annually, gross margin remains above 30%, the logistics center opens in Q4 2026 and supports frozen/chilled category expansion, and operating profit compounds toward NIS 100M+ over several years [Assumption]. Stock fair value: $38-$45 [Assumption].

Base case:

Sales grow mid-single digits, operating margin remains around 11%-13%, financial income normalizes lower, and the logistics center improves capacity but not immediately margins. Fair value: $28-$35 [Assumption].

Bear case:

FX turns adverse, food import costs rise, logistics-center delays continue, security conditions disrupt operations, and financial-income gains reverse. Operating margin falls below 10%. Fair value: $20-$25 [Assumption].

Stress case:

Regional war materially disrupts imports/distribution or damages consumer demand and supply chains. Liquidity disappears in the stock, and the market applies a severe country/microcap discount. Downside below $18 [Assumption].

## SECTION 4.5. Horizon Arbitrage

WILC's horizon edge is that many investors ignore small, illiquid foreign microcaps even when they are profitable and cash-rich. Patient investors can sometimes earn attractive returns when operational compounding is overlooked.

The opposite risk is that illiquidity is not a mispricing but a permanent limitation. A small Israeli food distributor may never receive a premium multiple from U.S. investors, especially during regional conflict.

Horizon conclusion: watchlist-worthy, but entry price matters. The stock must compensate for liquidity and geopolitical risk.

## SECTION 5.5. Behavioral

Bullish trap: overvaluing the cash balance without asking whether minority shareholders will receive it through dividends/buybacks or productive reinvestment.

Bearish trap: dismissing the company solely because it is small and Israeli. The operating results are real and profitable.

Liquidity trap: assuming a quoted market price equals executable value. With very low volume, position sizing and limit orders are essential.

Accounting trap: treating securities revaluation gains as recurring operating earnings.

Behavioral rule: only buy with a limit order and require a margin of safety.
