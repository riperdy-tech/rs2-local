## SECTION 0. Analysis Overview

Tyler Technologies is a high-quality vertical software company serving state, local, school, and public-sector clients. The business is not flashy, but it has several traits RS2 should like: mission-critical software, low customer turnover, durable recurring revenue, SaaS migration tailwind, public-sector domain depth, strong free cash flow, and a cleaner balance sheet after repayment of the 2026 convertible notes.

At $318.10, TYL looks meaningfully more attractive than many quality software peers. The headline GAAP P/E is about 44x, but 2026 guidance calls for non-GAAP EPS of $12.50 to $12.75 and free cash flow margin of 26% to 28%. On the midpoint of guidance, the stock trades at about 25.2x forward non-GAAP EPS and roughly 19x to 20x forward free cash flow. That is not "cheap" in an absolute sense, but it is reasonable for a business with high recurring revenue, public-sector durability, and double-digit recurring/ARR growth.

My view: TYL is one of the cleaner RS2 candidates so far. The stock has quality, valuation support, and multiple ways to win: SaaS conversion, margin expansion, share repurchases, tuck-in M&A, and continued cross-sell into a broad public-sector installed base. I would buy gradually, with a preference for adding on weakness before Q2 earnings.

## SECTION 1. Macro Context

Tyler's end market is public-sector software, which behaves differently from enterprise software sold to commercial customers. State and local governments, courts, schools, tax offices, public safety agencies, and municipal departments do not modernize instantly, but once systems are selected, they tend to remain in place for long periods. Budgets can be slow, procurement can be bureaucratic, and implementations can be complex, but churn is structurally low.

The macro backdrop is supportive but not risk-free. Governments continue to modernize citizen services, payments, courts, public safety, permitting, tax, appraisal, and administrative workflows. Cloud adoption remains a long-duration tailwind because many public entities still run legacy or on-premise systems. Tyler's SaaS transition turns that modernization pressure into recurring revenue growth.

The risk is that public-sector budget stress can delay new projects. Inflation, labor costs, pension burdens, property-tax politics, and federal funding changes can all affect IT spending. Tyler is not immune to procurement delays, but its software is often mission-critical rather than discretionary. That gives the company more resilience than most general-purpose software vendors.

## SECTION 1.5. Base Rate Check

The base rate for vertical market software with mission-critical workflows, low churn, and public-sector specialization is strong. These companies often compound for long periods because the customer pain points are persistent, product replacement is risky, and domain-specific depth matters more than generic software polish.

Tyler's reported client turnover is historically around 2% annually, and recurring revenue was 87% of 2025 revenue and 87.8% of Q1 2026 revenue. That is the signature of a durable vertical software model. The company also has a credible multi-year migration path from maintenance/on-premise licenses toward SaaS subscriptions, creating both revenue visibility and margin opportunity.

The base-rate caution is valuation and implementation complexity. Public-sector software companies can look safer than they are when investors ignore project delays, contract disputes, transaction-revenue exposure, and acquisition integration. Still, the base rate is favorable when entry price is reasonable. At roughly 25x midpoint 2026 non-GAAP EPS guidance, TYL clears that hurdle better than many quality-growth names.

## SECTION 2. Business Quality

Tyler describes itself as the broadest integrated portfolio of mission-critical software and services exclusively focused on the public sector. Its products support courts and justice, public safety, enterprise ERP, appraisal and tax, civic services, payments, digital government, student transportation, and related public administration workflows.

The business has three important quality pillars.

First, the customer base is sticky. Public entities do not casually rip out core court, tax, public safety, financial, permitting, or payment systems. Procurement is painful, migration risk is high, and internal users need continuity.

Second, Tyler has a scale advantage in a fragmented market. The company reported more than 45,000 successful installations across 15,000 locations. That gives it references, implementation experience, product breadth, and cross-sell opportunities competitors may lack.

Third, the SaaS migration is still active. Q1 2026 SaaS revenue grew 23.5% to $222.4 million, and Tyler has now reported 21 consecutive quarters of 20% or greater SaaS growth. Maintenance revenue declined 3.5% in Q1 2026, which is not a problem by itself; it reflects the shift of on-premise clients to SaaS.

The quality score is high because the company combines software economics with public-sector durability. It is not a hypergrowth SaaS story, but it is a strong compounding story.

## SECTION 2.5. Adjusted Financials

2025 results showed steady execution. Total revenue was $2.332 billion, up 9.1%. Recurring revenue surpassed $2.0 billion, grew 12.5%, and represented 87% of total revenue. SaaS revenue grew 20.6%. ARR grew 10.9% year over year. GAAP net income grew 20% to $315.6 million, or $7.20 diluted EPS. Non-GAAP net income was $495.5 million, or $11.31 diluted EPS. Free cash flow was $620.8 million, up 8.0%, with a 26.6% free cash flow margin.

Q1 2026 remained solid. Total revenue was $613.5 million, up 8.6%. Recurring revenue was $538.6 million, up 10.4%, and represented 87.8% of total revenue. Subscription revenue was $429.8 million, up 14.6%. SaaS revenue was $222.4 million, up 23.5%. ARR was $2.15 billion, up 10.4%.

Profitability was healthy. Q1 GAAP operating income was $99.8 million, up 11.9%; non-GAAP operating income was $166.6 million, up 10.0%; GAAP net income was $81.2 million, or $1.88 diluted EPS; non-GAAP net income was $133.4 million, or $3.09 diluted EPS; and adjusted EBITDA was $177.3 million, up 9.3%.

Balance sheet quality improved. As of March 31, 2026, Tyler had $316.0 million of cash and cash equivalents plus $81.8 million of available-for-sale investments. It repaid the $600 million convertible senior notes in March and had no outstanding borrowings under the 2024 credit agreement. The company had $700 million of available revolving credit capacity. It also repurchased about $250.1 million of stock in Q1 and another 298,144 shares after quarter end, with approximately $653.4 million of authorization remaining as of April 29, 2026.

## SECTION 3. Valuation

At $318.10 and approximately $13.41 billion of market cap, Tyler's enterprise value is roughly $13.0 billion using Q1 2026 cash and investments and no debt. This does not fully pro forma the April $223 million For The Record acquisition or post-quarter repurchases, but it is close enough for valuation framing.

LTM revenue is approximately $2.381 billion, calculated as 2025 revenue of $2.332 billion plus Q1 2026 revenue of $613.5 million minus Q1 2025 revenue of $565.2 million. That implies about 5.5x EV/LTM revenue. On 2026 guidance midpoint revenue of $2.555 billion, EV/revenue is around 5.1x.

The more interesting valuation is cash flow. Guidance calls for a 26% to 28% free cash flow margin on $2.535 billion to $2.575 billion of revenue. At midpoint revenue and 27% FCF margin, implied 2026 FCF is around $690 million. That puts TYL at roughly 19.4x market cap to forward FCF and 18.9x EV to forward FCF, before considering additional buybacks.

The non-GAAP EPS guide of $12.50 to $12.75 implies roughly 25.2x midpoint forward non-GAAP EPS. That is a reasonable entry multiple for a mission-critical vertical software company with durable recurring revenue, especially if FCF can compound toward Tyler's 2030 target.

Base-case fair value: $365 to $390, using roughly 28x to 30x forward non-GAAP EPS as the market rewards recurring growth and margin durability.

Bull-case fair value: $450 to $500, if SaaS growth stays above 20%, organic growth improves excluding the Texas payments drag, FCF margin holds near the high-20s, and buybacks shrink the share count meaningfully.

Bear-case fair value: $245 to $270, if recurring growth slows, payments/transaction revenue disappoints, public-sector budgets weaken, or the market assigns a lower 20x to 22x non-GAAP multiple.

## SECTION 3.5. Second-Order Effects

The first-order thesis is recurring revenue and SaaS migration. The second-order effects make the story more attractive.

First, SaaS migration can be margin accretive over time after cloud-transition costs are absorbed. Tyler's CFO specifically cited revenue mix improvement, cloud efficiency gains, and disciplined expense management as drivers of Q1 margin expansion.

Second, the decline in maintenance revenue is not necessarily bearish. It can be a sign that on-premise customers are converting to SaaS. The key is whether SaaS growth more than offsets maintenance decline, which it did in Q1.

Third, Tyler's payments and digital government assets create a transaction layer. Transaction revenue grew 6.4% in Q1 2026, but growth was 13.8% excluding the Texas payments contract impact. That means reported growth understates underlying demand in some periods.

Fourth, capital allocation is now a bigger part of the thesis. Repayment of the convertible debt removed a balance-sheet overhang, and the $1 billion buyback authorization matters at this valuation. Buying back stock around the low-$300s can be value-accretive if FCF compounds as guided.

Fifth, the For The Record acquisition adds AI-enabled court transcription capabilities to Tyler's justice portfolio. It is not thesis-changing on its own, but it reinforces Tyler's strategy of adding specialized public-sector workflow depth.

That creates a horizon opportunity if the investor looks through temporary reported-growth noise and focuses on recurring revenue, SaaS growth, and FCF conversion.

The 3-to-5-year thesis is stronger: public-sector cloud migration, cross-sell, AI-enabled workflow products, payments, and disciplined M&A can compound steadily. The stock does not require heroic growth assumptions to work from the current price.

The horizon arbitrage is that TYL is boring in the useful way. It may not excite momentum investors, but patient investors can benefit from the durability of the installed base and the cash-flow conversion.

## SECTION 5.5. Behavioral

The behavioral trap with TYL is boredom. Investors often prefer faster-growing or more obviously AI-linked software names, while Tyler quietly compounds in public-sector workflows. That can create opportunity when the valuation resets.

The other behavioral trap is over-reliance on non-GAAP metrics. Tyler's non-GAAP earnings exclude share-based compensation and acquisition-related amortization. Those are real economic considerations. However, FCF conversion is strong enough that the non-GAAP framework is not merely cosmetic.

A third trap is reading maintenance decline as churn. In Tyler's case, maintenance decline can reflect successful SaaS migration. The correct metric is total recurring revenue, SaaS growth, ARR, and retention, not maintenance in isolation.

## SECTION 7. Risks

Public-sector budget risk: local and state governments can delay projects when budgets tighten, political priorities shift, or procurement becomes more cautious.

Implementation risk: Tyler sells complex systems into mission-critical workflows. Delays, scope disputes, or client dissatisfaction can affect revenue timing, margins, and reputation.

Transaction revenue risk: payments and digital-government transaction volumes can be affected by contract changes, client behavior, fee structures, and macro activity. The Texas payments contract impact is a reminder that reported growth can be distorted by contract-specific factors.

Cybersecurity and data risk: Tyler handles sensitive public-sector data. A major breach, outage, AI misuse issue, or vulnerability could have severe reputational and financial consequences.

Competition risk: Tyler competes with specialized vendors, in-house systems, legacy incumbents, and large software companies such as Oracle, Workday, Salesforce ecosystem partners, and other public-sector or ERP providers. Its domain focus is a moat, but not invulnerability.

M&A risk: For The Record and future acquisitions can add growth, but integration mistakes can dilute margins or distract management.

Valuation risk: even after the pullback, TYL is not a deep-value stock. If growth slows below expectations, the multiple can compress.

## SECTION 7.5. Correlation Risk

TYL has lower fundamental correlation with high-beta enterprise software than the market may assume, because its customers are public-sector entities and its products are mission-critical. However, the stock still trades as a software multiple asset. In a broad software derating, TYL can fall even if operating results remain healthy.

It also has correlation to interest rates. Higher rates can pressure software multiples, even for companies with strong cash flow. The positive offset is that Tyler now has no outstanding borrowings and generates net interest income, so the balance-sheet impact is far less concerning than the valuation impact.

Portfolio implication: TYL is more defensive than many SaaS stocks operationally, but not immune to software-sector multiple compression.

## SECTION 9. Red Team

The bearish case is that TYL is a mature, slower-growth software company using SaaS migration and non-GAAP metrics to sustain a premium multiple. Total revenue growth was 8.6% in Q1 2026, which is good but not extraordinary. If the market refuses to pay a software multiple for high-single-digit growth, the stock can remain stuck.

The second bearish argument is that public-sector sales cycles are long and implementations are hard. Tyler's domain specialization is a strength, but it also means growth is not frictionless. Project delays can hit professional services, license timing, and client satisfaction.

The third red-team point is that FCF quality must be watched. Tyler's free cash flow is strong, but investors need to account for share-based compensation, acquisitions, and the capital required to keep products competitive. If buybacks merely offset dilution rather than create per-share value, the capital-return thesis is weaker.

The fourth red-team point is that AI could be double-edged. Tyler can embed AI into public-sector workflows, but AI also raises governance, security, privacy, and procurement concerns for government clients. Adoption may be slower than technology investors expect.
